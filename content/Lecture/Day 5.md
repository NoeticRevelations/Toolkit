# Group-based LI interventions, Collaborative Care & Ongoing Clinical Practice Development

## Who are psychoeducational groups suitable for?
Patients who have similar presentations, so that content remains relatable.
- Those who have some motivation to change
- Those who have clear goals 
- Those who are willing to complete homework tasks outside of sessions 
- Those whoa re able to understand the CBT model.

## What makes an effective group?
They are: 
- Co-facilitated (Morrison, 2001)
- Enables a sense of relatedness between patients 
- Active participation in intervention/behaviour change between sessions is promoted
- Risk is managed appropriately 

Examples: 
Pulmonary rehabilitation (Halding, Wahl & Heggdal, 2010) 
- Engagement in the group provided: 
	- Sense of belonging 
	- Opportunity to share knowledge 
	- Increase motivation for self-care and social participation 
	- Positive impacts on coping and adaptation

However some challenges encountered were: 
- Not ‘fitting in’ or being different 
- Being intimidated by group members who dominated conversation 
- Missing sessions – impacting cohesion within group

Within NHS TT:
- Imagery & Metaphor – easy to remember new concepts
- ‘Normal’ Language – jargon free
- Interactive – patients felt personally involved and able to participate as much or as little as desired
- Plan, Do, Review – Opportunity to feedback progress each session to emphasise importance of being proactive

Challenges:
- Recruiting enough patients that want to go to a group and who are likely to find it beneficial
- Keeping the sessions relevant for patients with a variety of LTCs (and mental health problems)
- Meaning for individuals being part of that particular group (“I don’t fit in” vs. “there are others like me”)

## Good Facilitator Skills (McKimm, J., Jollie, C. (2007)
- Enthusiasm
- Demonstrates and understanding of the subject
- Can conceptualise the topic
- Has empathy 
- Sessions are well structured with a clear agenda
- Understands how people learn
- Alert to context and events in room.

# Collaborative Care to Support Mental Health needs of LTC patients 

Traditionally, Health & social care services not organised in a way which supported an integrated response to both mental and physical health needs of patients. Usually pathways and services focus on single disorders of categories of health. Furthermore, there was separation between mental and physical health embedded within: 
- Training
- Payment Systems 

## Why is collaborative care important? 
- NICE specifically recommended CC for supporting depression in context of LTCSs in primary care. 
- Collaborative care improves both MH and PH outcomes - can't ignore one to treat the other

## What actually is Collaborative Care?
Collaborative Care should include: 

- Case Management which is supervised and has support from a senior mental health professional 
- Close collaboration between primary and secondary physical and mental health services. 
- A range of interventions including patient education, psychological and pharmacological interventions and medication management. 
- Care and follow-up 

## What does good TT-LTC care look like? 

- “My MH problem will be treated with the same urgency, compassion and respect as my physical health problem.”
- “I know that if I need a psychological intervention, I will be able to start treatment at a time that is right for me.”
- “I will jointly develop a care plan that outlines my treatment and includes self-management strategies. This will be kept up to date as my needs change.”
- “I will have access to clear and practical information regarding my treatment options. Where a range of evidence-based treatments are available , I will be able to choose the best option for me.”

## Barriers to Collaborative Care?

I often use the NHS Service Finder for staff quite useful - it allows staff to find contact details of teams and services, most often for GPs. It often has the GP surgeries direct number/emails for professionals so you can skip waiting on hold on the phone: https://digital.nhs.uk/services/nhs-service-finder


## Practical tips for creating joined up care?
- Empower the patient - drawing on their strengths and knowledge. 
- Benefits of involving informal carers/relatives of the patient, can they support the patient with the above. 

# A Brief introduction to neurological conditions 

## What is a neurological condition? 
Any condition that affects the brain, spinal cord, and or nerves. These control the way our mind and body are connected, they directly affect the way we think, feel and interact. 

Over 600 known types although 4 main categories used: 
- Sudden onset: Stroke, TBI, Spinal Injury, Meningitis or Gullian-Barre Syndrome. 
- Intermittent - Epilepsy, Migraine or Cavernoma
- Progressive - Parkinson, Dementia, Motor Neurone Disease, Ataxia. 
- Stable but changing - Cerebral Palsy, Tourette's, Narcolepsy, Fibromyalgia, Transverse Myelitis. 

These conditions can have a range of impacts on life: 
- 80% describe their QOL impacted to great or moderate extent.
- 78% described it affected their day-to-day activities to moderate or great extent.  
- 57% described it causing them pain to great or moderate extent. 

Consider this too: 
- those who identify as female, and those living in more deprived areas were more likely to report greater experience of pain, greater impacts on quality of life and greater impacts on day-today activities 
- 34% said their neurological condition made their mental wellbeing much worse.

## Experience of care
- 20% reported they were not given an explanation at diagnosis. 
- 31% said it wasn't handled sensitively 
- 79% said they had been offered a care and support plan but 35% described not being as involved in developing it as they would have liked. 
- 60% of adults said they hadn't been asked about their mental wellbeing by a HCP. 
- 30% told us they would like to be signposted or referred to support for their MHW but haven't 
- When asked about MH support 61% described it as making them feel better/more positive.
